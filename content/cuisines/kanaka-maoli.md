Kalo, or [[Taro]], is honored as an elder brother. It grows in loʻi, terraces flooded with water led from streams, and pounded and fermented into [[Poi]], it's part of almost every traditional Hawaiian meal. People show it respect: no harsh words while planting or eating it, and the poi bowl is covered if business comes up at the table.

Food traditionally came from the ahupuaʻa, land divisions running from the mountains to the sea. Upland forests gave ʻulu, [[Breadfruit]], and gardens gave ʻuala ([[Sweet potato]]), sugarcane and maiʻa (banana), while stone-walled loko iʻa, fishponds, raised fish along the shore, fed partly with kalo peels. Community groups are restoring loʻi kalo and fishponds on Maui, Molokaʻi and Oʻahu, and growers are planting far more ʻulu.
